# Fair Book Structure

The architecture of the revised Custer County Fair Book: how it is organized, how rules are numbered and cited, and how a rule is written. Doctrine for the mission lives in `charter.md`; this file is the book's own skeleton.

**Structure is closed as of 8/11/2026.** This file is downstream of the locked structure, not an authority over it. It records what was decided. It is not re-argued at the top of a session, and it is not used to reopen settled architecture. Sessions read it to know how to write, not to decide whether to write that way.

## The five structural rules

1. **Chapter-based.** Every project lives in a chapter. A reader goes to their chapter and stops.
2. **Rack to the top of the chapter.** Commonalities within a chapter rise to that chapter's shared layer. Project specifics stay in the project's subchapter.
3. **Numbering and lettering.** Chapter, subchapter, rule, then letters for subparts.
4. **Addendum for volatile facts. Reference, not reprint.** Dates, times, fees, names, and forms live in the Addendum. Rules are cited by number, not printed twice.
5. **Lean on CSF and IAFE constantly.** The norm is referenced wherever possible. County language rides inside the reference.

Everything below elaborates these five. Nothing below overrides them.

## Chapters

Every chapter's shared layer sits at the top of that chapter. CC 3.1 is what every market project obeys; CC 3.3 is what swine adds. A superintendent can be handed one numbered section and it is genuinely theirs.

| | Chapter | Sections |
|---|---|---|
| **CC 1** | Definitions and Application | who the book binds, every defined term, how to read a rule |
| **CC 2** | Rules and Regulations | named subchapters, divided by who a rule reaches |
| **CC 3** | Market Livestock | 3.1 all market projects · Beef · Swine · Sheep · Goat · Poultry · Rabbit |
| **CC 4** | The Livestock Sale | sale, terminal delivery, Wholesome Meat Act |
| **CC 5** | Horse | standalone |
| **CC 6** | Non-Market and Small Animal | 6.1 all non-market · Rabbit · Poultry · Dog · Cat · Llama · Breeding and Dairy |
| **CC 7** | Showmanship | 7.1 all showmanship · Livestock · Master · Companion Animal Master |
| **CC 8** | Shooting Sports | standalone |
| **CC 9** | Family and Consumer Sciences and General Projects | 9.1 general · project sections |
| **CC 10** | Open Division | 10.1 general · twelve divisions |

CC 5 and CC 8 are standalone: single-project chapters with no internal commonality layer to rack to.

Schedule and roster are gone from the book entirely; they are Addendum content. Book order departs from the current county order, which places Horse and non-market between the market departments and the sale. Reader logic wins over legacy order, and the change is walkthrough-visible rather than silent.

## CC 2 and its subchapters

CC 2 is **Rules and Regulations**. It divides into **named subchapters by who the rule reaches**. Three are known:

- **All Exhibitors** — true for every exhibitor in the fair, without exception.
- **Animal Exhibitors** — true for every exhibitor with an animal.
- **Open Exhibitors** — true for every Open Division exhibitor.

The set is **not closed**. Subchapters are created as the source requires them. Three are what has surfaced so far, not a fixed architecture.

The test for CC 2 placement: **a rule belongs in a subchapter only if it is true for every person that subchapter names.** A rule true for most but not all does not rise; it stays with its projects. Forcing a reader to check whether a CC 2 rule actually applies to them defeats the chapter structure.

A reader's path is: CC 1, their CC 2 subchapters, their chapter. Nothing they need lives anywhere else.

## Racking

Commonalities rack **within a chapter**, to that chapter's top. A rule shared by every market project goes to CC 3.1 and does not climb to CC 2. A rule shared by every animal project goes to the Animal Exhibitors subchapter of CC 2. A rule shared by every exhibitor goes to All Exhibitors.

Deviations and specialties stay in the project's own subchapter. Where a project genuinely differs, that difference is printed there and is not normalized into the shared layer.

## Numbering

```
CC 3          chapter        MARKET LIVESTOCK
CC 3.3        subchapter     Swine
CC 3.3.2      rule           the citable unit
CC 3.3.2(a)   subpart        a condition inside that rule
      (i)     sub-subpart    rarely, only when (a) itself lists
```

Rules cite three levels deep. **The fourth level is letters, never a fourth decimal.** Conditions inside a rule are subparts, so a penalty rule with four conditions prints as 2.1.8(a) through (d), not 2.1.8.1 through 2.1.8.4.

The **CC prefix is load-bearing**. This book prints CSF rule references on nearly every page by design. Without a county tag, a parent cannot tell whose Rule 4.2 they are reading. Ours are visibly ours.

**Bullets are banned in rule text.** A bullet cannot be cited, so anything a family can be held to carries a number or a letter. Bullets survive only in non-binding material such as a what-to-bring box. If it is a bullet, it is not a rule, and everyone can tell at a glance.

## Reference, not reprint

**A rule prints once.** Everywhere else it is cited by number.

Each project section opens with an **applies-to-you index**: every rule reaching that project, by number and title, in about one screen. The index points; it does not restate.

Reprinting a rule in full is an exception, not the standard. It has to be argued for at the pass where it comes up and its reason logged in the delta. Every reprint is a drift risk taken on deliberately.

This supersedes the prior standard, which required consequence-bearing rules to print in full in every location they reached.

## Volatile facts

Dates, times, fees, form names, rosters, and the schedule never print in the book. They live in the dated Addendum with fixed section numbers that never renumber across years. The book carries a pointer.

The Fair Book carries a version number only, never a year. Its number moves only on an adopted rule change.

## Leaning on the norm

**The book should be constantly referencing CSF and IAFE.** The default posture is that a Custer rule sits inside a CSF or IAFE shell. A rule standing outside a shell is the exception that has to justify itself.

**The flow:** reference the adopted rule first, then bring the Custer rule in as an example that helps explain it. Where the county rule deviates or narrows, make the reference, then the county specifics.

Pattern: *Custer County adopts and recognizes IAFE Item 5, including the prohibition on ice packs and refrigerants to alter finish, and on altering the color or structure of hair or wool except by trimming and blocking.*

The reference and the county specifics run in one continuous statement. Where the adopted text is itself printed in the book, county additions may instead carry a **Custer Exceptions** block beneath the rule.

Where the fit is strained, the county rule is drafted inside the shell anyway and flagged for the working group to screen. The drafter does not pull a rule out of a shell on their own judgment.

## Citation

- **CSF cites by heading name.** The CSF General Competition Requirements carries no section numbers, no rule numbers, and no section symbol anywhere. It runs on named headings. So: "Custer County acknowledges and adopts the Colorado State Fair General Competition Requirements, Determination of Violations." A parent can open the state's document, find that heading, and confirm the words match. Verified against source 8/10/2026.
- **CSF abbreviates to "CSF"** in the book, defined at first use in CC 1.
- **IAFE cites by item number.** Its eight guidelines are numbered, so "IAFE National Code of Show Ring Ethics, Item 7" is a real citation. The IAFE Code itself is an Addendum instrument, signed, and is referenced in the book but never restated.

## How a rule is written

Official-sounding and loophole-resistant pull in the opposite direction from complicated. Loopholes do not live in simple sentences. They live in vocabulary drift.

- Short sentences, active voice, a named actor. "The exhibitor must weigh in on Thursday," never "weigh-in shall be accomplished."
- **Must / must not** is mandatory. **May** is permitted. **Will** is the fair promising something. "Should" never appears.
- Every weight-bearing term is defined once in CC 1 and used identically forever. No synonyms, ever.
- Numerals for anything countable, so a kid can scan for the number.
- A rule with a consequence prints the consequence. A rule with a check prints who does the checking.
- Rule text targets a fifth-grade reading level.

What makes the book feel official is the citation format, the defined terms, and consistent typography, not the sentences. A parent who wants to argue is stopped by a rule that says exactly one thing, not by a rule that says it in six clauses.

## Two registers

Adopted text stays in its source's voice, formal, because it should sound like the source it came from. Custer exceptions are written plain, because they are the county talking directly to a family about what the rule means for them. A reader can tell at a glance which is which, and learns fast that the plain paragraphs are the ones telling him what to actually do.

The pattern is explained once in CC 1.

Audience is eight through eighteen, with enough weight that the rules sound official, because it is the parents who hunt loopholes.

## Pass sequencing

Shared layers are built before the projects that lean on them. A reconciliation sweep runs at the end of each chapter to catch commonalities the project passes surface that the chapter's shared layer missed.
