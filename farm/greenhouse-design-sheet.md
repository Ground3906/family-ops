## MISSION

Four-season cold-hardy greenhouse. Never freezes inside. Greens, roots, brassicas through a Westcliffe winter. Produces into Chow Hall in the months when nothing else does.

**Two-phase build.** Phase 1: livestock barn, roughly two years, absorbing the garage-barn conversion program. Phase 2: vacate animals, convert to greenhouse with structure already standing.

## SITE

Built-up fill platform, roughly 6 ft proud of surrounding grade. **Building sits on grade on all four sides.** Ground is higher to the east; fill falls away quickly to the south and west beyond the footprint. No wall is buried. No retaining condition anywhere in the building.

Wheel-packed in lifts with the Gehl approximately 2023, three freeze-thaw cycles since. Not a tested Proctor number.

Power and water already on site. Gas reaches easily.

No shading. Confirmed by owner observation. No county building code jurisdiction.

## LOCKED ITEMS

### Item 2 - Use
Four-season, cold-hardy. Never freezes inside. Passive solar carrying the load, mechanical heat as insurance.

### Item 3 - Footprint
18 ft 6 in x 40 ft, 740 sq ft. Container is the north wall. Building runs south off the container's full 40 ft face.

### Item 4 - Container layout
As above. Fixed and closed to design.

### Item 5 - Coop / greenhouse air relationship
Wall stays sealed and insulated. **One-way air path: greenhouse to coop.**

Coop already runs an exhaust fan. Fan pulls, makeup air enters wherever it can. Today that is cold outdoor air through windows. With the greenhouse attached, makeup air is drawn through the existing 20 in x 30 in window from a heated sunlit space instead.

Coop stays negative pressure relative to the greenhouse for as long as the fan runs. Coop dust and ammonia cannot migrate into the greenhouse. Separation is enforced by pressure gradient, not filtration. No new hardware.

**5.1 locked:** greenhouse window is primary intake. Exterior coop windows retained as functional backup. A coop should never have exactly one way to breathe.

### Item 6 - Structure geometry
**Single-slope shed.** Framed riser built up off the container top, sloping down south. Peak sits over the north wall against insulated mass, not under glass. Warm air collects there.

### Item 7 - Floor
No floor drop. Building sits on the pad as-is. Earth-tempering (walipini logic) does not apply: a fill berm standing proud is not stable ground.

**Slab under the pens only. Individual pens. Dirt on the remainder.** Animals locked in pens at all times, no free roaming. Walking happens outside the greenhouse. This contains two years of manure to a scrapeable surface and prevents nitrogen and ammonia saturation of future bed soil.

**Pen slabs are poured separately and later**, independent of the structural foundation. No structural dependency.

### Item 8 - Foundation
**NO STEM WALL.** Monolithic perimeter footing with post piers cast integral, one continuous pour. Top of footing 6 in proud of finished grade.

| Element | Spec |
|---------|------|
| Perimeter footing width | 16 in |
| Depth | 12 in minimum, 16 in better |
| Post pads | ~30 in square, cast integral with the perimeter |
| Top of footing | 6 in above finished grade |
| Reinforcement | Two #4 continuous, chairs, corner bends |
| Anchor bolts | 0.5 in x 10 in, 6 ft o.c., within 12 in of each corner |
| Frost skirt | 2 in rigid foam, 4 ft out horizontal, 12 in below grade, wider and thicker at corners |

**Why monolithic:** separate piers and a separate perimeter heave independently at 9,000 ft and tear the wall apart. One pour, one element, everything moves together.

**Why 6 in proud:** keeps the treated plate out of splash and drift, gives the frost skirt a clean termination, and gives a hard line to flash siding down to. It is not a stem wall, just a taller footing form.

**Frost protection is the whole defense.** With no stem wall, the frost skirt and slab-edge foam are all there is. Frost-Protected Shallow Foundation detail, heated-building case. The building is heated, so the standard heated detail applies.

**Slope crest setback:** footing set 2-3 ft back from the slope break on the south and west, minimum. A footing too near the crest shears the wedge out from under itself.

**Differential settlement:** north end bears on the container (long settled), south end on fill. Polycarbonate does not forgive racking.

**Load is light.** Post loads at 50 psf are ~5,550 lb each. Bearing capacity of the wheel-packed fill was never the constraint.

### Item 9 - Bed placement
**Beds move inboard. South strip under the glass becomes walkway.**

Light through a horizontal window band lands in a strip from 1.84x sill height to 1.84x head height, measured out from the wall. **The strip width is constant regardless of sill height; raising the sill slides the lit strip north, it does not shrink it.** A tall sill therefore throws light past a bed sitting under the glass.

With a walkway under the glass and the bed inboard in the lit strip, sill height costs nothing but reach.

**Winter lit strip at the locked 4 ft 2.5 in sill:** approximately 7 ft 8 in to 12 ft 11 in south of the north wall.
**Summer beam reach:** roughly 1 ft 3 in. Vertical south glass self-shades hard, no curtain, no operational decision.

**A waist-high bed can nest under the south glass** (30 in bed under a 4 ft 2.5 in sill, 20 in clearance). It will not receive direct winter beam - it sits inside the dark zone. It gets diffuse light, wall bounce, roof poly light, and full sun March through October. Shade-tolerant winter crops only: lettuce, spinach, chard, mache. Nothing fruiting. It is also the warmest spot in the building. Treat it as extra capacity, not the winter workhorse.

**Bed heights, general:**

| Height | Type |
|--------|------|
| 12-18 in | Standard raised bed, kneel to work |
| 24-30 in | Waist height, stand to work |
| 34-36 in | Bench height |

Four-season work in January argues for the taller end. Cold air pools at the floor, so getting the root zone up is a thermal advantage at altitude. Usual compromise is 24-30 in total height with 12-18 in of soil depth.

## STRUCTURE

### Post and beam south wall

The wall does not carry the roof. Posts and a continuous beam do. This is what makes the window framing trivial: headers over openings become non-structural infill.

| Element | Spec |
|---------|------|
| Bays | **4 at 10 ft 0 in** |
| Posts | 6x6, five total, galvanized standoff bases |
| Clear between posts | 9 ft 6 in |
| Window rough opening | 7 ft 9 in - fits with 21 in of framing |
| **Beam** | **6x12 solid Doug fir or larch**, two 20 ft lengths spliced over the center post |
| Studs between posts | 2x6, 16 in centers, non-bearing |
| Window header | 2x6 racked to the top plate, carries nothing |

**NO ENGINEERED LUMBER ANYWHERE IN THIS BUILDING.** LVL, glulam, LSL and PSL are interior-dry-use products. Greenhouse humidity, condensation dripping off a cold beam, and repeated wet-dry cycling will delaminate them.

**Built-up beams (triple 2x12) have the same problem in miniature** - three plies face to face trap water in the seams and rot from the inside. A solid timber has no glue lines and no seams, and dries and breathes as a unit. Rough-sawn local Doug fir or larch is cheaper and dimensionally full (a rough 6x12 is a true 6x12, not 5.5 x 11.25).

Green timber checks and shrinks as it dries. Normal, not structural. **Bolted connections need re-tightening after the first season.**

**Posts must not sit directly on concrete.** Galvanized standoff bases lifting end grain an inch clear. Concrete wicks water and a post end sitting in it rots from the bottom in a wet building.

### Beam sizing at 50 psf

| Input | Value |
|-------|-------|
| Ground snow load | **50 psf (Westcliffe town)** |
| Roof dead load | 10 psf |
| Rafter span | 18 ft 6 in |
| Tributary width | 9 ft 3 in |
| Uniform load on beam | ~555 lb/ft |
| Load per 10 ft bay | ~5,550 lb |
| Required section modulus | ~64 in3 |
| 6x10 provides | 83 in3 - works |
| **6x12 provides** | **121 in3 - specified** |
| Post load each | ~5,550 lb |

**6x12 specified over 6x10 deliberately.** Rough-sawn green timber from a local mill is not graded to the certainty of kiln-dried dimensional stock. 6x10 gives a 30 percent margin; 6x12 gives 90 percent. The price difference is not where to economize on a snow-loaded roof.

Steel (W8x15 or W10x15) rejected on cost. Fabrication, coating and setting equipment exceed timber at this span.

### Rafters

| | Spec |
|---|---|
| Size | **2x12** |
| Spacing | **16 in o.c.** |
| Count | 31 |
| Stock length | **20 ft** |
| Bearing span | 19 ft 8 in |
| Bearing north | Birdsmouth on riser doubled top plate, 20 deg |
| Bearing south | Birdsmouth on the 6x12 beam, 20 deg |
| Ties | Hurricane tie at every rafter, both ends |
| Blocking | Solid 2x12 between rafters at both bearing points |

2x10 does not make this span at either 50 or 60 psf. **Span drives the size, not the load.**

**Overhang framed separately.** 20 ft stock covers the 19 ft 8 in bearing span with nothing left for a tail. Frame the south overhang as sub-fascia and outlookers after the roof is on. Two feet at the south eave shades the top of the south glass in summer without touching winter sun and keeps runoff off the windows and footing.

**VERIFY 20 FT 2x12 AVAILABILITY BEFORE THE DESIGN DEPENDS ON IT.** At most yards this is a special order, not stock. If painful to source, the alternative is 8 ft bays with an intermediate beam line: more posts, shorter rafters, all common stock.

### Bearing assignment

| Wall | Bearing? |
|------|----------|
| North riser | **Yes** - rafters bear on top plate |
| South | **Yes** - via posts and beam |
| **West** | **Yes** |
| **East** | **NO - non-bearing** |

**East is non-bearing by design.** Easternmost rafter set 12-16 in inboard of the east wall; roof cantilevers or is carried out on a lookout ladder to the fascia. This drops every header in the east wall to nominal 2x6 and eliminates the spanning member that would otherwise be needed over the door-window stack.

**Non-bearing does not mean unloaded.** A 14 ft end wall on the Sangre front takes full lateral wind pressure across a face that is mostly glass and door. Structural sheathing required, transferring to the roof diaphragm and the foundation.

**Shear panel minimum width is roughly 1:3.5 against height.** On a 12 ft wall that is a 3 ft 6 in minimum continuous panel. Measure what solid wall actually remains between the east wall openings and at the corners. If a 3 ft 6 in panel cannot be found, sheathing alone will not do it and a portal frame or Strong-Wall is required. **This may move the door.**

Sheathing replaces let-in bracing entirely. Metal T-strap bracing is the substitute when a wall gets non-structural exterior; it is not needed here. Hurricane ties and hold-downs are separate hardware for a separate failure mode (uplift and overturning, not racking). **This building wants both.**

### Riser detail (north wall, on the container)

**Load path is the whole story.** A shipping container carries load through its four corner castings and its steel side rails. The roof panel is 14-16 gauge corrugated and carries almost nothing. A rafter set down in the middle of that roof dents it.

The riser stands on the **top side rail** - the continuous structural C-channel running the full 40 ft along the container's south edge.

Bottom up:

1. **Continuous steel angle sill.** L3x3x0.25 or heavier, bolted through the top side rail every 24 in with backing plates. Bolting preferred over welding to avoid burning the paint.
2. **Treated bottom plate** on the angle, sill seal or butyl between. Wood never touches steel dry: condensation on cold steel rots untreated lumber and corrodes the rail.
3. **2x6 stud wall, 16 in centers, 5 ft 0 in tall.** Doubled top plate.
4. **Rafters birdsmouthed to 20 deg** on the top plate, hurricane tie each.
5. Sheathing, weather barrier, metal siding.

**Three details that matter more than they look:**

- **Uplift.** The angle-to-rail connection is the hold-down for the entire roof. Bolt it properly.
- **Thermal break at the rail.** The steel angle runs inside to outside. 2 in closed cell over the steel, inside, lapping the rail, or a 40 ft condensation line forms at the joint. **This is the highest-value square footage on the job.**
- **Vent panels land in the top 2 ft of this wall.**

## ROOF

**10 ft polycarbonate south, 10 ft metal north. Two panels down-slope, one lap seam, no cuts.**

### Polycarbonate

| | Spec |
|---|---|
| Product | **8 mm triple-wall polycarbonate** |
| Panels | **10 ea, 4 ft x 10 ft** |
| Coverage | 400 sq ft |
| Flutes | **Run down-slope** so condensation drains out the bottom |
| UV face | **Out.** Multiwall has an up side and a down side |
| Fastening | **Purlins, not solid decking.** Oversized holes, gasketed screws |
| Expansion | ~0.25 in per 10 ft across the temperature swing. Undersized holes buckle and crack panels |

**R-value caution.** 8 mm triple-wall packs three skins into 8 mm of depth, so the air gaps are thin. Expect roughly R-1.9 to R-2.0, not the R-2.5 of 16 mm triple-wall. Better than 8 mm twin-wall (R-1.6). **Verify against the manufacturer data sheet at order; multiwall performance varies by brand and rib geometry.**

Flutes running crosswise is the classic mistake and it fogs the panel permanently.

### Metal, north 10 ft

Solid sheathing, closed cell foam, metal panel. This section gives an insulated deck to hang lights, fans, or a curtain track from, and turns the tall bay into a dark insulated reservoir rather than a night-time heat loss.

**The roof is two different assemblies on the same rafters.** North 10 ft: sheathing, foam, metal. South 10 ft: purlins and poly, no sheathing, no foam, open to the space below. The transition lands at the lap seam.

### Why 10 ft of poly

Winter ray trace: light entering the roof more than about 7 ft horizontal from the south edge overshoots the floor and lands on the north wall, which is foam-insulated and cannot store it. **North roof glazing contributes nothing in winter.**

10 ft is chosen over the 8 ft the ray trace suggests because it is exactly one panel with no cut and no waste, and it moves the glazing ratio from 65 to 76 percent on the poly alone. The extra two feet buys shoulder-season and summer light (high sun lands where it enters) plus diffuse light on cloudy days, which does not care about angle.

Past 10 ft the trade turns bad. 12 or 14 ft buys shoulder light paid for every winter night.

### Phase 1

**Roof the entire slope in metal for the animal years.** Polycarbonate's UV warranty clock (typically 10 years) starts the day it goes up, and livestock humidity and ammonia attack the UV coating. At conversion, pull the south 10 ft of metal and drop poly in. Framing is laid out for that seam from the start. Metal comes off in one piece for another building.

### Snow shed minimums

| Condition | Minimum pitch |
|-----------|---------------|
| Heated poly | 20-22 deg |
| Unheated poly | 28-30 deg |
| Heated metal | 18-20 deg |
| Unheated metal | 25-30 deg |

Locked 20 deg sits at the heated-poly minimum. Panel seams and exposed fasteners want a few degrees more. Melt-refreeze at 9,000 ft creates eave ice dams that pitch alone does not solve.

## INSULATION

**CLOSED CELL ONLY. NO OPEN CELL ANYWHERE.** Open cell is vapor-permeable and absorbs moisture. In a greenhouse it becomes a wet sponge, loses R-value, and rots the framing behind it. 2 lb closed cell is R-6.5 per inch and at 2 in is its own vapor retarder and air barrier.

**Timing:** foamer comes after framing, sheathing, roof, windows and doors are in. Before any interior finish. Everything dry and enclosed.

| Surface | Depth | Result |
|---------|-------|--------|
| North riser | 5.5 in | R-35 |
| Steel angle + container top rail | 2 in over the steel, inside, lapping the rail | Kills the 40 ft condensation line |
| West wall | 5.5 in | R-35 |
| Roof, north 10 ft metal | 5.5 in to underside of deck | R-35 |
| **South wall, below sill** | **3.5 in** | R-22 |
| **East wall, solid areas** | **3.5 in** | R-22 |
| Rims, plates, corners | 2 in over everything | Seals bridges |
| **Roof, south 10 ft poly** | **NOTHING** | - |

**Why 3.5 in and not 5.5 in on south and east:** you cannot insulate a wall that is mostly glass. Both walls are broken up by openings with R-2 glass directly above the framing. Filling those cavities to R-35 spends money on the strong link while the glass governs the wall's real performance. 3.5 in of closed cell is still a full vapor and air barrier.

**Where that money goes instead: the footing and slab edge.** 40 ft of concrete on frozen ground with nothing between them is a bigger uncontrolled loss than 2 in on the south studs.

**Tell the foamer explicitly:**

1. **Do not spray the south 10 ft of roof.** That is the phase-two poly section. Foam there means demolition later. **Mark it on the deck before he arrives.**
2. **Do not spray over the poly-to-metal lap seam.**
3. **Do not skip the container top rail.** Steel inside to outside is a guaranteed condensation plane.

**Foam is permanent.** Frame the phase-two poly opening completely before he shows up or you will be cutting foam off rafters in two years.

## INTERIOR FINISH

**Snow Coat white elastomeric over the foam. No interior metal panel.**

Interior Pro-Panel was considered and killed on cost. Drywall was raised and immediately killed: paper facing and a gypsum core in an 80 percent humidity building is a mold farm.

| Zone | Surface |
|------|---------|
| Bottom 4 ft, animal contact | **OSB wainscot, temporary** |
| Everything above | **Snow Coat, 2 coats** |

**Why elastomeric works here:** goes on thick, stays flexible, cures bright white. Over closed cell it seals the foam against UV and wear. On lumber it seals against moisture without trapping it. Highest-reflectance surface available, which is what carries the north-zone light bounce.

**Why it needs the wainscot:** elastomeric is soft by design, easy to scuff and gouge, and not a wash-down surface. It will not survive animal contact or a pressure washer. OSB wainscot carries the animal years and comes out at conversion. **Nothing permanent gets built for a two-year problem.**

**Fire rating is not a factor.** No code jurisdiction. Intumescent coating (DC315, Fireshell) was discussed and is not required.

**Application:**

- **Trim the foam flush first.** Closed cell comes out lumpy and proud of the studs. Coating will not hide it and it is being sprayed, not hung over. Budget a scraping pass - labor, not material, but a real day.
- **Two coats minimum.** One coat over tan foam reads dingy and kills the bounce being paid for. The first pass drinks more than label rate.
- **Airless only**, 3,000+ psi, 1.0 gpm minimum, 0.031 to 0.041 tip, 3/8 in hose minimum. Graco 390/490 class is the floor; 795 or ag-duty is what 1,250 sq ft in two coats actually wants. Rental.
- **Do not thin past the label.** Elastomeric performance is a film-thickness property.
- **Flush immediately.** It sets up in the hose and gun.
- **Temperature window:** roughly 50 F and rising, no freeze for 24-48 hours. At 9,000 ft that is a summer window, building closed in first. **Confirm against the Gardner-Gibson data sheet before renting.**

## VENTILATION

**Fully passive. No power, no controls, no thermostat.** Wax-cylinder openers on both ends: a heat-driven piston expands and pushes the panel open, contracts and lets it fall shut. Responds to actual temperature, not a schedule. Nothing to wire, nothing to program, nothing to fail at 9,000 ft in February.

### Riser exhaust - LOCKED

**Intake at 1.5x exhaust is deliberate.** Intake air moves slower than exhaust; the larger opening keeps flow from choking at the entry. Stack venting stalls entirely without low intakes - high vents alone will not flow.

Stack height between intake and exhaust is roughly 10 ft.

**Intakes are NOT placed between the windows.** The piers between bays offer only ~2 ft of width, and a tall narrow intake running to 7 ft lets air enter high and short-circuit straight to the riser vents without ever touching the growing zone. The below-sill band is already the right height and has the area, and cutting the piers would weaken the shear wall.

**Gravity louvers were rejected.** They open on airflow. Stack pressure is very low - an inch of water column or less - and cheap gravity louvers may never crack open. A wax cylinder pushes regardless of pressure.

### Panel construction, both locations

| | Spec |
|---|---|
| Skin | Pro-Panel, **ribs horizontal, parallel to the hinge** |
| Core | Rigid polyiso, foil-faced |
| Adhesive | **Foam-board-safe only** - Loctite PL300, DAP Foamboard, or PU. Solvent-based glue dissolves EPS |
| Frame | Aluminum angle perimeter, **drip edge at the bottom rail** |
| Gasket | **EPDM or silicone bulb, mounted on the FRAME, not the panel** |
| Cutting | **Nibbler or shears. Never an abrasive blade** - it burns the galvanizing and the edge rusts within a year |

**Why ribs must run horizontal.** The panel is 4 ft wide, supported only along the top edge, and bends about the hinge line. Ribs parallel to the hinge put stiffness where the bending is. **And vertical ribs crossing the bottom rail create channels the gasket cannot close** - corrugated gaps at the edge that has to seal tightest. Horizontal ribs land flat on the gasket across the full width. Water in the horizontal channels leaves at the drip edge.

**Why top hinge outswing on both.** The panel is its own rain hood. Gravity assists closing, so the wax cylinder only has to push - which is exactly the load it is designed for - and a failed cylinder fails **closed**. Panels sit outside the building, so no walkway conflict.

**Polyiso preferred over EPS:** higher R per inch, and the foil facer gives adhesive something real to grab. **Do not hinge into foam** - hinges and gasket land on the perimeter frame.

**VERIFY WAX CYLINDER LIFT AGAINST FINISHED PANEL WEIGHT BEFORE ORDERING.** An insulated metal panel is heavier than a typical greenhouse vent, and the cylinder must push it from dead closed against a compressed gasket.

**Two exterior consequences of low outswing intakes:** drifted snow against the south wall can block a panel or open it into a drift, and eight panels hanging open at knee height are catch points for the Gehl and for animals. Matt has accepted both.

## DESIGN NOTES CARRIED FORWARD

- **Sill height does not change how much light you get. It changes where the light lands.** The lit strip is constant width; raising the sill slides it north. This is why beds moved inboard.
- **Panel length sets depth.** Not the reverse. Slope length always exceeds footprint depth on a pitched roof.
- **Framing cost is per opening, not per square foot.** This killed the small cooler doors.
- **The riser translates to sill one for one**, with no effect on pitch, depth, ratio, or structure. It has bailed out three separate constraints in this design.
- **Container is already white and already insulated.** It needs nothing. "North wall" in structural notes means the riser only.
- **The tall bay is a feature.** Warm air collecting at the peak against insulated mass under a metal roof is a reservoir and an ideal intake plenum.
- Chinese-style solar greenhouse (two-plane south face, steep glazed wall, insulated night curtain) remains a live alternative. Passed over, not ruled out.
